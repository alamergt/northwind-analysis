Question 1 · Sales leadership

Which markets grew or declined, which accounts drove it, and where should sales attention go?

Why it matters. Sales attention is limited. This shows where extra effort is likely to pay off, and where revenue hangs on a single customer.

Key finding

Revenue grew +80% (between +42% and +116% depending on the quarters compared). Germany clearly gained share; Central Europe, France and Other North America clearly lost it. Brazil, UK & Ireland and Nordics change direction depending on the quarters compared.

Business interpretation

Germany (QUICK-Stop) and UK & Ireland (Hungry Owl All-Night Grocers) rely on one customer for about half their revenue (50%–52% and 43%–53% depending on the period), so their concentration risk is borderline. The clearest losses trace to single accounts: Mère Paillarde (−$15.2K), Blondel père et fils (−$10.5K) and Piccolo und mehr (−$8.5K).

Recommended action
  • Invest in Brazil (tentative)
  • Broaden Germany
  • Broaden UK & Ireland (tentative)
  • Rescue Central Europe: keep Ernst Handel close, recover Piccolo und mehr and widen the customer base (tentative)
  • Diagnose France and Other North America
  • Diagnose Nordics (tentative)

How this was analysed

Revenue is compared between the same two quarters a year apart (Q4 1996 + Q1 1997 against Q4 1997 + Q1 1998). Each market is measured on the change in its share of company revenue, so a market growing exactly as fast as the company scores 0%, and on its largest customer's share of the market. Outperforming means +15% or more relative to the company; underperforming means −10% or less; high concentration means one customer holds over 50%.

Performance levels are directional. Each market has only about 13 to 42 orders per six-month window and a few large orders move the totals, so no market's level is statistically certain (see the uncertainty table in the appendix). The direction of each market's performance holds in every robustness check.

How this was analysed

Recent demand is units ordered in the last 90 days, as a monthly rate. Months of stock is stock divided by that rate. Critical: out of stock, or under half a month even counting stock on order. High: under a month on hand, with too little on order. Watch: below the reorder level, or thin cover without a reorder rule or with erratic demand. Within each tier, products are ranked by the monthly revenue at stake.

This is a priority list, not a forecast. The stock snapshot has no date and is assumed to be taken at the analysis cutoff (6 May 1998), and there are no supplier lead times, so the list ranks urgency but can't predict when a product will run out.

Question 3 · Operations

How do processing times and freight costs vary across shipments, and where are the biggest opportunities to improve logistics?

Why it matters. Late dispatches cost customer goodwill and freight is a cost on every order. This shows whether to fix Northwind's own process or renegotiate with carriers.

Key finding

Dispatch takes 6–7 days for nearly every order. 14-day orders are not processed any faster, so 18% of them go out late (95% range 10%–28%), against 4% for 28-day orders.

Business interpretation

The problem is a small number of very slow orders in Northwind's own dispatch process, which are more common for United Package and Speedy Express shipments. Carrier freight comparisons are inconclusive after adjusting for order size and destination, so the data doesn't support changing carriers on cost.

Recommended action
  • Sort the dispatch queue by required date
  • Find out why United Package and Speedy Express shipments more often wait over 18 days (e.g. pickup schedules)

Full interpretation

The problem is a small number of very slow orders in Northwind's own dispatch process, which are more common for United Package and Speedy Express shipments. Carrier freight comparisons are inconclusive after adjusting for order size and destination, so the data doesn't support changing carriers on cost.

  • Typical dispatch takes 6–7 days for every carrier, region and order size. The problem is a small number of very slow orders (79 took more than 18 days), not slow processing in general.
  • Orders with a 14-day deadline are not processed any faster (median 7.5 days, against 7 for 28-day orders), so 18% of them go out late (95% range 10%–28%; 12 of the 37 late dispatches). Sorting the dispatch queue by required date is a low-cost change aimed at these.
  • United Package and Speedy Express shipments are more often among the very slow orders (13% and 9% of their orders, against 6% for Federal Shipping). Their typical dispatch time is the same once those orders are set aside. The data doesn't show why: it could be pickup schedules or which orders are assigned to which carrier.
  • Among employees, orders handled by Margaret Peacock and Janet Leverling add the most lost order-days (they handle many orders), and Anne Dodsworth's orders are most often slow (21% of them). A look at how those orders reach the warehouse could help.
  • 21 orders ($25.9K) placed in the last four weeks hadn't shipped by the cutoff; none is past its required date, so this looks like normal work in progress.
  • Carrier freight comparisons are inconclusive once order size and destination are taken into account (differences anywhere from −11% to +34%), so this data doesn't support renegotiating or switching carriers on cost.
How this was analysed

Processing time is days from order to dispatch for the 809 shipped orders; the 21 unshipped orders are reported separately. Groups are compared only with orders of similar size, and each group's gap is measured against the best comparable group. A regression checks whether carrier differences survive once order size, destination and employee are taken into account.

Processing time ends when Northwind dispatches the order; delivery dates aren't in the data, so carrier delivery performance can't be judged. Freight is assumed to be a cost to Northwind. Opportunity sizes are gaps against the best comparable group in the same order-size band, not guaranteed savings.

What this analysis can't tell us

  • Profit: there are no product costs, so everything is about revenue.
  • Cause: the comparisons show where things differ, not why.
  • Delivery: dates record dispatch, not delivery.
  • Timing of stock-outs: the stock snapshot is undated and there are no supplier lead times.

Data that would close these gaps: product costs, carrier delivery dates, dated stock records, supplier lead times, sales territories and freight terms.

Technical appendix

About the data and caveats
  • 1996 and 1998 are part-years, so growth compares the same quarters a year apart.
  • Most countries place about one order a month, so smaller countries are grouped into regions.
  • Only 5 customers placed their first order after mid-1997: changes in revenue come from existing accounts.
  • The stock snapshot has no date and there are no supplier lead times: the restocking list is a priority order, not a forecast.
  • Dates record when Northwind dispatched an order, not when it was delivered.
  • There are no product costs, so everything is about revenue, not profit.

Independent review

A reviewer that hadn't seen the conclusions recomputed the headline claims from the raw workbook and tried to break them. Each objection and how it was resolved:

  • Q3: typical dispatch time (high): The page said the median was 7 days everywhere, but Federal Shipping and very large orders have a median of 6 days. Resolution: Confirmed and corrected: the page now says 6–7 days.
  • Q3: 97% of late dispatches are slow orders (medium): This is close to automatic: any late order with a 28-day window took more than 28 days, so it is slow by definition. Resolution: Confirmed; the claim was removed. The late rate for 14-day orders is reported instead, with its 95% range.
  • Q3: carriers dispatch 1.3 and 1.8 days slower (high): The gap comes entirely from the slowest 10% of orders, and 'carriers dispatch slower' implies a cause the data can't show. Resolution: Confirmed: without the slowest orders the gaps are 0.4 and 0.2 days and not material. The page now says these carriers' shipments are more often among the very slow orders (13% and 9% vs 6%), that the data doesn't show why, and the regression without slow orders is in this appendix.
  • Q3: freight differences not material, so renegotiating isn't supported (medium): The intervals are wide (about −5% to +34%), so the honest result is inconclusive rather than 'no difference'. Resolution: Accepted: the page now calls the freight comparison inconclusive and gives the range.
  • Q3: 14-day orders not prioritised (low): A higher late rate follows partly from a shorter window; the real evidence is that their median processing time is no faster. The sample is small. Resolution: Accepted: the median comparison (7.5 vs 7 days) now comes first, with the 95% range of 10–28%.
  • Q1: dependence on one customer (high): Dependence is measured over a different window from growth. Over the compared periods, QUICK-Stop is 50% and Hungry Owl 43%, below the 50% line. Resolution: Confirmed. The approved rule (latest four quarters) is kept, but the page now gives both figures and calls Germany's and UK & Ireland's concentration borderline.
  • Q1: share gains and losses (high): Directions depend on the quarters compared; Brazil, UK & Ireland and the Nordics flip. Resolution: Confirmed with a new three-period check (in this appendix). The key finding now names only the markets that move the same way in all three, and actions for the others are marked tentative.
  • Q1: invest in Brazil (high): Brazil is 'not conclusive' in the bootstrap check, its direction flips, and 76% of its gain is one customer. Resolution: Confirmed; the action is now marked tentative, with the reason given.
  • Q1: company growth +80% (low): The figure depends heavily on which quarters are compared. Resolution: Accepted: the page gives the range (+42% to +116%).
  • Q1: rescue Central Europe (medium): Revenue grew and the largest customer grew; the share drop is mainly one lapsed account. Resolution: Already corrected before this review (the page says Ernst Handel isn't the main cause and names Piccolo und mehr). Methods and definitions
  • Revenue is price charged × quantity × (1 − discount), counted by order date.
  • Relative growth is the change in a market's share of company revenue: 0% means it grew exactly as fast as the company.
  • Concentration is the largest customer's share of the market over the latest four quarters; above 50% is high.
  • Recent demand is units ordered in the last 90 days (6 Feb–6 May 1998), as a monthly rate.
  • Months of stock is stock ÷ recent monthly demand.
  • Processing time is days from order to dispatch. Slow orders are the slowest 10%.
  • Opportunity is excess days (or freight dollars) per order compared with the best comparable group in the same order-size band, times the group's orders.
